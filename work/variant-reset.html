Case study 03 · Tim Hortons · Mobile ordering

Variant Reset: the slowest option was the right one.

In the Tim Hortons app, changing an item's size quietly wiped every customization a guest had made. For someone who had removed cheese for a dairy allergy, that meant the wrong order and no warning. I led the fix end to end: I designed a study that put three ways of handling the reset in front of every participant, recommended the slowest one because it was the only one guests could trust, and took it through a phased release. 16 of 20 participants preferred it, and guest-care tickets about order accuracy fell almost 20% week over week.

Act I

The mess.

A bug everyone knew about

Guests who customize their orders, removing allergens, adjusting portions or choosing alternatives, expect those choices to stick. In the Tim Hortons app they didn't. If a guest changed the size or variant of an item after customizing it, the app reset every modification back to default. No warning, no confirmation.

The bug was well known across the team, and we agreed it had to be fixed. What made it urgent was who it hurt most.

  1. 1 · CustomizeA guest removes cheese from a sandwich because of a dairy allergy.
  2. 2 · Change sizeThey switch to a different variant of the same sandwich.
  3. 3 · Silent resetThe app puts the cheese back. Nothing tells them, and they order it.

Order accuracy was already the top source of guest complaints, and this was a significant driver. App-store reviews called out "modifiers disappearing" and "changes not sticking".

Why it needed more than a quick fix

Because a reset could expose guests to allergens they had explicitly removed, this carried safety and legal implications, not just a usability cost. The solution needed sign-off from directors of Product, Design and Engineering before it could ship.

The real questionNot whether to fix it, but how to tell guests about a reset in a way that protects them without slowing ordering down.

Act II

The meaning.

Designing the right test

I designed a remote usability study on Dscout to compare three ways of handling the reset. Rather than giving each version to a separate group, I used a within-subjects design: each of the 20 participants tried all three, in a randomized order so no version benefited from going first.

When the same person tries every option, their feedback is a direct comparison rather than an average across different people. For a decision that would need director sign-off, that made the evidence much harder to argue with.

Each participant did two tasks per version: customize a brewed coffee (two creams, two sugars and a vanilla shot), then change it from a medium to a large. Afterwards, they told us which notification style they preferred.

Why the slowest option won

On timing alone, Silent looked best: fastest, and rated easiest. But speed wasn't the right measure here, and when participants were asked which they preferred, the answer was clear.

  1. Silent was the bug. It removed the friction and the transparency with it. A guest with a dairy allergy had no way of knowing their choice had been cleared. The absence of a message doesn't communicate safety; it communicates nothing.
  2. Toast was easy to miss. It informed guests, but participants' feedback showed that a message that disappears on its own wasn't enough for someone who needed to be certain what had happened to their order.
  3. Alert gave guests control. It was the slowest, 23 seconds against 12, but the only version that asked before changing anything. Participants called it "attention-grabbing" and "clear", and 16 of 20 preferred it. They felt informed, not surprised.

I recommended Alert, framing the 11 extra seconds as a deliberate trade-off rather than a cost to minimize. Directors of Product, Design and Engineering agreed.

The right answer wasn't in the speed data.

What shipped

The alert appears only when a guest has customized an item and then changes its variant, so guests who haven't customized anything never see it. It covers every way into the customization screen (the menu, Order Again and editing from the cart) for pickup and delivery orders. It explains what will happen and lets them decide, instead of silently overriding their choices.

In the live app

  1. Customize. The guest picks Double Double and adjusts their dairy.
  2. Change the size. Before anything resets, the alert asks: "Reset customizations?"
  3. Decide. Cancel keeps everything. Continue resets the item, and the guest knows to check it.
  4. Customize again, knowingly. No silent surprises in the order.

Act IV

The proof.

A careful release

Given the safety context, we rolled out behind a feature flag so anything unexpected could be pulled back before it reached every guest. Each phase had to clear the same bar: no severe bugs, no escalations from guest care or restaurant support, fewer guest-care tickets, and minimal impact on conversion and speed.

  1. Jan 30 – Feb 6Regression testing in staging before any guest saw it
  2. Feb 9–1010%, then 50% of guests: no new bugs, no spike in complaints
  3. Feb 12, 2026100% of guests, with no rollbacks

There was no impact on conversion or ordering speed, and no negative app reviews: the alert added a moment of awareness without slowing people down. The more important outcome is trust. Guests with allergies or dietary restrictions can now change an item's size without worrying that the app has quietly overridden their choices.

Learnings

What I'm taking with me.

  1. Speed and safety pull in different directions, and you have to choose deliberately. The research didn't make the decision for me. It gave me the evidence to make it confidently, and to defend it to leadership.
  2. Qualitative feedback fills the gaps numbers can't. On timing alone, the existing behaviour looked best. Participants' reactions explained why Alert was the right call. If I'd only looked at the numbers, I'd have shipped the wrong solution.
  3. Bring stakeholders into the research, not just the readout. When I presented the results, I spent time getting people to trust the data. Watching a participant try to work out what happened to their selection is more convincing than any slide. Next time I'd invite them into the sessions.

What's next

From safety net to no net.

The alert solves the awareness problem: guests now know when their customizations are about to be reset. The better long-term answer is modifier persistence: keeping a guest's customizations automatically when they change variant, wherever the new variant supports the same options.

That's a harder engineering problem, because variants don't always share the same modifier sets, which is why it wasn't in scope for this release. But it's the right next step.

The alert is a safety net. The goal is to not need one.
