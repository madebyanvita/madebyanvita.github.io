Case study 04 · Tim Hortons · Mobile ordering

Order Again: the feature we chose to remove.

The Tim Hortons app gave returning guests two ways to reorder: Recents and Favourites. Usage data showed one was doing the job and the other was adding work. With my PM, I made the call to remove Favourites and rebuild reordering as a single Order Again experience. After rollout, conversion rose from 59.1% to 62.7% and drop-off on reorder screens fell by 20%.

Act I

The mess.

Two tabs, one job

Returning guests are the most valuable guests in any ordering app. They already know what they want and they're already motivated to order. The only thing between them and a completed order is how quickly the app gets out of their way.

The Tim Hortons app gave them two ways back to what they love: Recents, a log of past orders, and Favourites, a list guests saved by hand. On paper both served the same goal. In practice they split one job across two tabs, asked guests to keep two mental models of their order history, and made Favourites a list guests had to curate to keep useful. Most weren't doing that work.

What success meant

Three outcomes defined success: one for guests, one for the business and one for the team maintaining the product.

  1. Reduce guest confusion. One clear, fast path to reorder instead of two tabs doing the same job.
  2. Make reordering faster. Fewer steps from intent to cart, measured in speed and drop-off.
  3. Streamline the codebase. Stop maintaining two parallel reorder systems and free engineering time for higher-value work.

The questions we set out to answerHow might we reduce clutter without removing value for guests who used Favourites? And how might Recents carry the best of both: convenience and speed?

Act II

The meaning.

The insight: your favourites are already in your Recents

My PM brought the usage data and we read it together, starting from one question: if two tabs do the same job, which one are guests actually using? The answer was unambiguous. Recents was used three times as often, and guests ordering through it finished faster.

That told us two things. Favourites wasn't delivering enough value to justify itself, and Recents was already doing Favourites' job, because the things guests truly love and reorder show up there on their own. We were asking guests to maintain a list the app could maintain for them.

Making the call

Removing a feature guests can see is a harder sell than adding one, so we weighed the case for keeping it honestly. The strongest argument was that Favourites could feel less cluttered for guests who reorder the same few items. Against that: 92% of guests weren't using Favourites regularly, the exceptions (seasonal items and discontinued limited-time offers) were edge cases, and keeping two parallel systems had a real cost in design and engineering time.

The PM and I made the call together: remove Favourites, and put all the design and engineering effort into making one reorder experience significantly better.

AfterFull menuOrder again

With one tab left, its name mattered too. Our copywriter and I proposed renaming it from "Recents" to "Order again": it describes what guests come to do, not how the list is built.

Act III

The work.

Four directions, before any detail

With Favourites gone, the question became what the new reorder list should look like. Rather than design one answer in isolation, I took four directions to the team early, each a genuinely different idea of how reordering should work, not a visual variation. Putting them side by side forced a real conversation about trade-offs instead of a review of one preferred solution.

The data settled it. About 115,000 guests a month (17%) added single items from their history, against 95,000 (14%) who re-added whole orders, so item-level reordering was the interaction to optimize. Direction B also gave the cleanest interface and the lowest engineering overhead. The guests who preferred whole orders didn't lose the ability: they could rebuild an order by tapping items. What they lost was a one-tap shortcut only 14% used.

A card that fits every product

Direction B lives or dies on one component: the item card. I explored fixed and adjustable heights, with and without a quantity, focusing on clarity, hierarchy and accessibility.

Card explorations, with spacing specs. Adjustable height without a quantity badge won.

An adjustable card sounds simple, but it has to hold every product name in the Tim Hortons catalogue, in English and French, without making the list feel unpredictable. Rather than guess, we plotted the length of every product name in both languages.

The finding: 97% of names fit within two lines, 88% are 43 letters or fewer, and 46% fit on a single line. That turned a design opinion into a defensible, data-backed spec for card height.

  1. Adjustable height. Most products need at most two lines for the name, and a flexible card handles the long tail without truncating everything else.
  2. Every modifier, shown in full. Seeing exactly what was in a past item prevents ordering mistakes.
  3. No quantity. Tapping + again is faster than adding a quantity control, and removing it kept the card and the checkout cleaner.

The quick-add card didn't stay a one-off: it became a reusable component in the design system, so other reorder and menu surfaces can use the same pattern.

Feedback you barely notice

The last detail was how to confirm an item had been added. The obvious answer was a toast sliding up from the bottom of the screen. We'd seen toasts tested on another feature, and for this one the team agreed it slowed things down: guests reordering are in a fast, intent-driven state, and even a small interruption breaks that momentum.

Instead, I explored micro-animations in Lottie: a small burst on the + button, haptic feedback, and the cart count ticking up in real time. The animation restarts on every tap, so adding the same item three times feels as quick as tapping three times. The team chose the third of my explorations.

The shipped flow

In the live app

  1. Open Order again. It sits next to Full menu, so returning guests are one tap from their usual.
  2. Tap + to add. Each tap adds the item with its saved modifiers, and the cart count ticks up with no toast in the way.
  3. Tap the item to change it. Tapping anywhere outside the + opens the item to customize before adding.
  4. Review the order. A few taps and the cart is ready.

Designing for the edges

A reorder experience is only as good as its worst moment, so I designed for the two states most guests will never notice.

New guests

Instead of a blank screen, the empty state explains what Order again will show and sets expectations for their next visit, turning a dead end into a small onboarding moment.

Unavailable items

Past items aren't always orderable: the restaurant may not offer them, the daypart may not allow them (no lunch at breakfast), or they may no longer be sold. I mocked up three ways to handle it: an "Item unavailable" tag on the card, a dismissible banner at the top, or hiding those items entirely.

After discussing it with the team, we chose to hide unavailable items for the first release: it kept the code simpler and meant guests only ever saw things they could order. The other two options stay ready if testing shows guests need to see what's missing.

The final spec covered the rest of the rules: identical items (including modifiers) appear once, the list draws on up to 50 orders from the last six months and shows at most 20 items, and it works across English and French in Canada and English in the US.

Learnings

What I'm taking with me.

  1. Removing a feature is a design decision too. Most portfolios show what was built. This project was defined by what we took away, and by the rigor needed to make that call responsibly. The results validated it: less drop-off and higher conversion than the experience it replaced.
  2. Passive convenience beats deliberate curation. Guests don't want to maintain a Favourites list; they want the app to remember for them. The speed gain didn't come from a clever interaction pattern. It came from removing work guests were never enthusiastic about doing.
  3. Small decisions compound. Card height, quantity, toast or micro-animation: none feels significant alone. Together they decide whether an experience feels fast and trustworthy or slow and uncertain. The detail work is the product.

What's next

Where this goes next.

  • Measure in detailTrack reorder rates and modifier retention, to know not just whether guests reorder but whether one-tap reordering with saved modifiers works as intended.
  • PersonalizeRecommend items within Order again by time of day, location or ordering patterns, once the core behavior is proven at scale.

The best feature is sometimes the one you don't ship. The second best is the one that does the work so your guest doesn't have to.
