Case study 03 · Tim Hortons · Checkout & payments

Mobile Checkout: two screens, one decision.

Checkout is where everything the Tim Hortons app does to win an order either pays off or falls apart. Ours had grown into separate payment and store-confirmation screens, on a front end that predated the design system. I led the redesign end to end. I ran a workshop that got 14 people from product, engineering and content to agree on how checkout should be structured, merged payment and store confirmation into one screen, and rebuilt it on design-system components. After launch, guests who reached payment completed their order 84.5% of the time, up from 74.7%.

Act I

The mess.

A checkout built up in layers

The Tim Hortons app is one of Canada's most-used quick-service restaurant apps, and checkout is where every order is won or lost. Over several iterations it had piled up friction: too many steps, inconsistent payment UI, and a confirmation screen that left guests unsure their order had gone through.

For a pickup order, a guest went from the cart to a payment screen, then to a separate screen to confirm the restaurant, and only then placed the order.

Three problems underneath

  1. A legacy front end. Significant business logic lived in the front end, and the flow had never moved to the new design system. That meant stability issues, slower development and longer fixes when something broke.
  2. Service modes almost nobody used. Dine-in and Curbside were under 1% of Mobile Order & Pay sales but carried a disproportionate share of logic and maintenance.
  3. A two-step checkout. Guests spent around 37 seconds across separate payment and store-confirmation screens, which raised the risk of drop-off at the most expensive point to lose them.

Together they slowed checkout, drove support tickets and limited how fast the team could modernize ordering.

Everything that could go wrong

Before redesigning anything, I catalogued every error state in the old flow, from unavailable items to declined cards and tip limits, so each one could be redesigned on the new components too.

What success meant

  1. One screen, not two. Merge payment and store confirmation into a single checkout step.
  2. Faster for returning guests. Cut end-to-end checkout time by at least 15%.
  3. Built on the system. Raise design-system coverage in the cart screens to 90% of UI elements.

The real questionNot what checkout should look like, but how it should be structured, and how to get people with very different priorities to agree on it.

Act II

The meaning.

Alignment first, as a design step

The existing flow had grown out of technical constraints and one-off decisions. Redesigning it alone would have meant defending my assumptions against everyone else's in every review. Instead, I brought the people who would approve, build and write checkout into one workshop to agree on structure first.

14 people took part: directors, product managers, engineering leads and content partners. Having decision-makers and the people who'd build it in the same room meant strategic and practical concerns surfaced together, not weeks apart.

I broke checkout into seven sections and asked each person to arrange them by what felt most logical to guests, what had to be confirmed before placing an order, and what belonged together.

What the room agreed on

  1. Location and method are one decision. 13 of 14 grouped where they order with how they get it. The old flow split them across screens. This finding is what justified merging store confirmation and service mode into one section.
  2. Upsell belongs next to the order summary. 9 of 14 grouped them. Guests are most open to add-ons while they're reviewing what they've chosen, so the suggestion helps instead of interrupting an earlier decision.
  3. Prep time comes right before payment. 9 of 14 put it there. Guests want to know when their order will be ready before they commit to paying for it.

How the best apps do it

Alongside the workshop, I reviewed checkout in Uber Eats, DoorDash, Starbucks and other ordering apps. It wasn't a one-off exercise: as each part of checkout took shape, I went back to see how others had solved that specific piece. The goal wasn't to copy patterns but to understand them: how top apps group store, service mode and payment; where they place upsell and rewards; how they make wallet-first checkout fast; and how they handle carts left behind.

The review confirmed the direction: the strongest apps consolidate steps, keep confirmation light and put wallets first. It also helped separate what was a real industry norm from what was just a constraint of our legacy flow. Patterns like a mini cart and an upsell before the cart were worth exploring too; they became concepts we weighed below.

I also mapped both legacy flows, pickup and delivery, screen by screen, so every requirement buried in the old screens was accounted for before anything was removed.

Act III

The work.

From a 1:1 rebuild to one screen

I didn't start with the answer. The first step was a 1:1 rebuild: every legacy screen mapped onto the new design system, keeping the same steps, so we had a lower-risk baseline and a complete inventory of what each screen did. Other structural explorations ran alongside it. The workshop came next, and it decided how the final flow should be structured. From there I reworked the UX and UI into the version that shipped.

Three ideas we didn't ship, and why

Alongside the 1:1 rebuild, we explored structural changes that would have pushed checkout further. All three were directionally strong. None fit this release, and being clear about that early kept the scope focused.

Concept A

  • A mini cart with its own upsellA light add-on moment before the full cart, to capture revenue earlier and declutter the cart.
  • Why notIt needed new cart-state handling and extra API orchestration, beyond the scope of the refactor.

Concept B

  • Choosing prep time on the payment screenAsk about timing at the moment of commitment. What shipped comes close: guests choose it on Review order and see it confirmed right above payment.
  • Why notIt depended on store and method APIs and backend sequencing, and would have needed refresh logic that risked destabilizing the flow.

Concept C

  • Pickup or delivery on the payment screenGroup the final decisions together and make the cart shorter.
  • Why notChanging method recalculates price, tax and availability. Moving it meant significant API rework and risked the payment logic.

The common limit was architecture: we had to keep the existing linear, one-screen-per-step flow, and the scope was improving the backend, not rebuilding it. They remain good ideas for when that changes.

Removing what guests didn't use

Dine-in and Curbside made up under 1% of Mobile Order & Pay sales but added logic to every order. As a group, we decided to remove them, simplifying both the logic and the choices guests see.

What shipped

Checkout became two screens: one to review the order, and one to confirm and pay. Both were rebuilt on design-system components, with editable sections, clear headers and inline validation, so guests can change the restaurant or service mode without losing progress. Here's how the workshop's findings show up in the pickup flow.

The shipped flow

  1. Add from the menu. The cart builds at the bottom of the screen, with the pickup restaurant always visible.
  2. Review order. Rewards, where and how to pick up, and when to start preparing, then the order and add-ons, in the order the workshop agreed.
  3. Add one more thing. Suggestions sit right under the order, and adding one confirms with a short message without leaving the screen.
  4. Place order. Pickup details confirmed right above payment, with Pay with card or Apple Pay. One screen, where there used to be two.
  5. Done. The order confirmation leads with the pickup name, then the restaurant, pickup method and the order.

Delivery follows the same structure. Tipping got its own short step between the two, as in the competitor flows we'd reviewed, so the place-order screen holds only what a guest needs to confirm.

  1. Wallet-ready. The place-order screen supports Apple Pay and Google Pay alongside saved cards, for near one-tap checkout.
  2. Carts that make sense when guests come back. I defined clearer rules for abandoned carts, so a returning guest doesn't hit a stale store or an item that's no longer available.
  3. A foundation engineering can move fast on. Less logic in the front end and shared components made checkout easier to update and test, which matters most when a payment issue needs a fast fix.

Designing the states, not just the screens

The order confirmation had to work for every combination of service mode and prep time, so I mapped each one.

  1. Pickup, ordered for later. The confirmation shows the time preparation will start, with a "Prepare now" option. Tapping it asks guests to confirm before the kitchen starts.
  2. Pickup, ordered for now. No choices left to make: the confirmation shows the pickup details, the order and a link to the receipt.
  3. Drive-thru. Guests can't change the pickup time. They go to the window and say their name, and the team member recalls the order from the point-of-sale system to start preparing it.

Once the main screens were settled, I redesigned the error states from the audit on the same components.

Reading the whole funnel honestly

Across the whole app, end-to-end conversion rose from 37.6% to 38.2%, and average time to order fell from 3.4 to 3.19 minutes, about 12 to 15 seconds faster. Those numbers are smaller because they include steps checkout doesn't touch. Over the same period, drop-off between the menu and the cart rose from 21.3% to 28.8%, well before a guest ever sees checkout.

One number inside checkout moved the wrong way: the step from cart to payment slipped from 88.1% to 86.8%. Everything after payment improved more than enough to make up for it, but it's the step I'd look at first.

Learnings

What I'm taking with me.

  1. Alignment is a design deliverable. The workshop settled the structure before any screen existed. Because 14 people had already agreed on the order, design reviews were about details, not direction.
  2. Measure the part you changed. End-to-end conversion is the number leadership asks for, but it blends in everything else happening in the app. Looking at the steps we rebuilt showed the real effect, and showed exactly where to look next.
  3. A clear "not now" is part of the design. Three strong concepts didn't fit the architecture. Being explicit about why, instead of forcing them in, kept the release focused and kept the ideas alive for when the backend changes.

What's next

Checkout is never done.

The results said the direction was right. The next iterations are about precision.

  • Swipe to delete, now liveSmall improvements to the cart, designed with engineering, like swiping to remove an item.

Checkout is a signal, not a finish line.
