Case study 01 · Tim Hortons · Self-serve kiosk

Rebuilding the kiosk, from vendor to pilot.

As the sole designer, I led the end-to-end redesign of Tim Hortons' in-restaurant kiosk: choosing the vendor, restructuring the menu around how guests think, and taking it to a live pilot. Within months, kiosks were taking 13% of front-of-house sales, up from about 10%.

Act I

The mess.

A rare opportunity

Tim Hortons' kiosks were overdue for a rethink. The vendor contract was ending, the 2019 interface looked visibly dated, and guest frustration with ordering was tangible.

That made room for a ground-up redesign: new vendor, new UX and new design-system alignment, across thousands of Canadian stores. I was brought in as the lead designer, with no design lead above me, and owned it end to end.

What I saw in the store

Before designing anything, I used the existing kiosk in a restaurant. The problems were immediate: low-resolution product images, typography that treated categories and items identically, and a drill-down folder structure that forced guests through several pages to find a single item.

Guests who didn't already know the menu were left searching, not ordering.

The folder approach assumed every guest already knew Tim Hortons' menu architecture. That became the central problem to solve.

What success meant for the business

Each priority answered something guests were struggling with or missing entirely.

  1. A cleaner look and feel. Retire the 2019 visuals, refresh product photography and fix French-language inconsistencies.
  2. Better upsell and discovery. Video splash screens, more prominent featured items and clearer modifier pricing.
  3. A simpler experience. Fewer taps to complete an order, fewer pages, effortless category switching.
  4. Loyalty that works. Scan for rewards at any point, not just checkout, and bring back redemptions disabled since 2023.

The design goalMake ordering at the kiosk feel as fast and familiar as ordering at the counter.

Learning from kiosks I could touch

With a tight timeline, I studied competitor kiosks in person at nearby McDonald's and Aroma Café locations, evaluating layout, payment flow and placement first-hand. McDonald's stood out for an efficient ordering flow and clear hierarchy; Aroma for a payment flow with minimal steps. Both shaped two decisions in the redesign: the tab navigation structure and a shorter checkout.

Act II

The meaning.

The most consequential call: folders or tabs

Keeping folders or moving to tabs would shape every guest interaction at every kiosk in Canada. Both had real trade-offs, so I designed complete flows for each and mapped them against our three goals: speed, discoverability and cognitive load.

Earning alignment

Stakeholders initially resisted leaving folders: they were familiar to the internal team, and change at national scale carried real risk. Opinion wasn't going to settle it, so I made a more rigorous case: card-sorting data on how guests actually think about the menu, folder navigation mapped against abandoned-order patterns, and a prototype test of both structures side by side.

It took two rounds of stakeholder review to align on tabs.

Testing with real guests

Before committing to the new structure, I ran a research program with cross-functional partners, combining card sorting and tree testing. Each question below was a real point of stakeholder pushback, and the research was designed to answer it with data.

  • Could guests navigate the current and proposed structures with ease?
  • How do guests think about ordering sandwiches, especially adding or removing proteins and modifiers?
  • Does a "protein-first" naming convention make items faster to find?
  • Can guests still find donuts when they're nested under Sweet Treats?
  • Do bagels stay findable once they're no longer a main category?
  • Can guests place complex orders with several modifiers efficiently?

Four findings that shaped the final design

  1. Make Vanilla Iced Coffee its own item. Guests got confused customizing iced coffee; listing it as a product streamlined selection.
  2. Rename "Espresso" to "Espresso-Based". A small change that helped guests find cappuccinos and Americanos they had been overlooking.
  3. Building sandwiches from base carriers felt too complex. Guests struggled to assemble from scratch, so the approach was flagged for re-evaluation once the menu was final.
  4. List bagels in two places. Guests looked for them under both Breakfast and Baked Goods, so double-listing, without a dedicated category, matched their mental model.

Act III

The work.

Mapping the whole experience first

With no design lead above me, I scoped the full experience from scratch. I wireframed three complete flows (ordering, checkout and payment, and loyalty) before designing a single high-fidelity screen, which surfaced structural problems and stakeholder gaps while they were still cheap to fix.

Choosing the vendor: Tillster or Acrelec

Alongside the UX work, I evaluated two competing vendors, Tillster and Acrelec, who had each proposed a design for the new kiosk. Rather than judging on aesthetics, I assessed both against three criteria.

  1. Design-system fitHow closely did the proposal follow our system?
  2. FlexibilityHow much room for future customization?
  3. Pricing at scaleHow did the cost compare across the network?

Tillster's proposal reused components from another client's build and didn't adopt our design system, which meant inconsistent spacing, typography and interactive states that would have needed significant rework. Acrelec matched our designs closely, showed more flexibility for customization and offered more competitive pricing. We chose Acrelec.

Design handoff is a relationship, not a document.

Keeping that quality with an external vendor took ongoing communication, shared standards and quick design-test turnarounds, not just a spec file.

A modifier model the business could run

How items are structured in the back end directly shapes what's possible in the interface. So designing customization meant working closely with Category Management, Menu and Pricing, and understanding the data model well enough to have informed conversations about what was feasible and what needed to change.

The goal for guests: clear options, minimal cognitive load, and enough flexibility for Tim Hortons' most complex items. The answer was a small set of reusable modifier scales, each with a sensible default.

The final experience

The happy path

  1. Start with what's new. New & Seasonal is the first thing guests see.
  2. Browse a category. Tabs switch sub-categories without leaving the page.
  3. Customize. Size and presets first, then a scale for each add-on.
  4. Review and pay. Every item shows its photo and modifiers before checkout.

Before and after, screen by screen

Menu. A screen of folders became items up front, with categories always visible.

Category. Another page of folders became tabs that switch in place.

Customize. One long list became size and presets first, then scales.

Review. Text-only line items became items with photos and their modifiers.

What Version 1 changed

Version 1 shipped to pilot stores in late 2024. Each change maps back to the business priorities, and anything that wasn't ready was deliberately held for Version 2 rather than squeezed in.

Look & feel

  • French languageFixed accents, capitalization and the text-to-image ratio of French copy.
  • AssetsRefreshed banners and product photography to match current packaging.
  • AccessibilityAccessibility and language options on every screen, with no restart needed.

Cheque lift

  • Attract loopA video splash screen that adapts by daypart, region and test market.
  • Featured homeGuests land on items they can order. The disposition page is gone.
  • NavigationTaps to purchase down from 5 to 3. No "select category" page; tabs filter sub-categories without going back.
  • PromotionsPromotions toggle by daypart and region, and limited-time offers deep-link to the product.
  • ModifiersClearer options, with step pricing shown up front.

Loyalty

  • Base loyaltyConfirmation when a guest scans at any point, not just checkout. Redemptions back after being off since May 2023.
  • Scan & PayHeld for Version 2.
  • Quick ordersHeld for Version 2.

Alongside the software, the new hardware brought faster internals, better scanners and optional status lights.

Act IV

The proof.

Watching it in the wild

When the first kiosk went live in a pilot store, I went in person to watch guests use it in a real environment, something no usability test can fully replicate. I also spoke with a few guests, which added depth to what I saw.

It showed me both where the experience needed refining and what was already working. Everything I saw went straight into the Version 2 backlog, so the next iteration was grounded in how the product actually performed, not how we expected it to.

Needs UX attention

  • Payment needed clear error states and feedback when a scan completes.
  • Guests read "Back" on the payment screen as "Skip".
  • Filters didn't read as interactive; guests scrolled to browse instead.
  • Guests weren't sure they could type their name: there was no cursor.
  • French Vanilla was hard to find.

Technical

  • Scrolling wasn't smooth.
  • Daypart mismatch: lunch was available at the counter before the kiosk.
  • Adding an espresso shot to brewed coffee didn't work.
  • A single cookie couldn't be bought, only packs.

Working as expected

  • Upsell: some PMs worried the overlay was too light and would confuse guests. Of 20+ guests observed, none were: each read it, chose "No thanks" and moved on to checkout.
  • Asked how it went, guests said "good" and "easy to use".

General

  • Guests were ready to pay from the cart; looking down at the scanner, hardly anyone saw the payment options.
  • After checkout, 50–60% weren't sure whether to wait for a receipt or where to stand.
  • Seeing staff use the kiosk made other guests want to try it.
  • At the lunch rush, some guests lined up for the kiosk by choice.

Pilot store observations, condensed. The full list fed the Version 2 backlog.

Closing the loop

  3. ShippedAcrelec added a blinking cursor to the kiosk.

Learnings

What I'm taking with me.

  1. Watching is worth more than asking. The pilot store surfaced friction that never appeared in controlled testing: guests scrolling past filters, payment options invisible because everyone was looking down at the scanner. You can't design a physical-world product from a desk.
  2. Vendor relationships are a design problem. Getting Acrelec to implement our design system, not their defaults, took the same skills as any stakeholder alignment: clear standards, early involvement and documentation they could actually use. A spec file isn't enough when the team implementing it has its own defaults and timelines.
  3. Bring stakeholders into the research, not just the review. Presenting conclusions after the research meant I spent time convincing rather than deciding. Next time I'd invite key stakeholders into live sessions: watching guests struggle with folders together is more persuasive than any slide.
  4. Instrument the funnel from day one. I'd push for per-screen drop-off and time-on-screen during the pilot. The data confirmed the direction was right, but finer-grained funnel data would have made Version 2 prioritization sharper and faster.

What's next

Work in progress.

Version 1 proved the core experience works. Version 2 is about making it work for everyone. In progress:

  • Scan & PayA seamless scan-and-pay experience that cuts wait times and gives guests more ways to pay.
  • Gift cardsSimpler purchase and redemption.
  • CombosA smoother "make it a meal" flow.
  • Continuous testingUsability testing in parallel, grounded in how guests use the kiosk in the wild.

The kiosk is a living product. 13% is a starting point, not a ceiling.
