Case study 06 · BMO · Digital banking

Sending money abroad, without a trip to the branch.

At BMO, only business clients could send money abroad online. Everyone else had to use a service like Western Union, go to a branch, or mail a bank draft. I joined on a six-month contract as the only designer on Global Money Transfer, a new feature for BMO's mobile and web banking. I designed the whole flow, worked within BMO's design system and accessibility standards, and helped a researcher run usability testing. We used what we learned to improve the flow, and the feature launched.

Act I

The mess.

Only businesses could do it

BMO's Pay a Bill team wanted to bring Global Money Transfer to everyday banking. Until then, only business clients could use it. Everyone else had to find another way: a service like Western Union, a trip to a branch, or a bank draft sent by mail.

The team believed BMO could do this better, right from the accounts people already use, and catch up with other banks that already offered it.

The questionHow might we let people send money abroad without going anywhere, using a third party, or relying on a courier?

Harder than paying a bill

Every country asks for different bank details. India uses an IFSC code, Europe uses IBAN, and other places use SWIFT or an ABA routing number. Exchange rates change, fees vary, and transfers take days. And because it's someone's money going to another country, people need to trust every step.

I was also working inside BMO's design system and accessibility standards as a contractor, so I joined their design syncs regularly to check the work against them.

The meaning.

Starting from what people had already told BMO

Before I joined, BMO's UX team had interviewed people who send money abroad, and I used what they found as my brief. That research, and the business case behind the feature, are internal to BMO.

Mapping the whole feature

As the only designer, I mapped every path before designing screens: how people find the feature, what they see the first time, how they manage contacts and rates, and what happens after they send. We designed mobile first.

The hardest part: adding a contact

This is where most of the complexity was. The form changes based on the country you pick, because every banking system identifies accounts differently. I designed one flow that adapts to the country, with a help tip for each code and a search to find the right bank.

Home, from day one to when things go wrong

The home screen brings together the rate watchlist, saved contacts and past transfers. I designed it for a first visit, when there's nothing there yet, and for when part of it doesn't load: that section shows its own message, and the rest of the screen still works.

Receiving money, too

Sending is only half of it. If someone abroad wants to send you money, they need your bank details. I designed a way to share them straight from the account details screen, by message, email or any app.

Testing it with real customers

While the team was building, I helped a researcher run moderated interviews and usability sessions on my Axure prototype. We wanted to learn how people already send money abroad, and to test our flow from start to finish.

We spoke to six people: three women and three men, aged 21 to 60, all in Canada. They all bank on their phones every month and send money abroad several times a year, using services like PayPal, Western Union, TransferWise, Remitly and other banks.

What we heard

  • Trust comes from confirmationPeople want to see their details confirmed, with check marks, emails or texts. Three said they double-check everything they type.
  • Rates get checked firstFive check the exchange rate on other sites before sending, and some will wait for a better rate.
  • Bank codes can stop a transferPeople who hadn't sent money to India didn't know what an IFSC code was. Everyone liked the search under the field, but two said they might stop and ask the recipient.
  • Tracking should feel like progressEveryone expected a progress bar or notifications after sending. Five check their email straight away, and three check with the recipient.

Where people got stuck

  • The tour looked realTwo people didn't realise they were in a tutorial, and two thought its sample contacts were real ones they could pick.
  • Who gets notified?"Notification method" wasn't clear: was it for their own alerts, or a message to the recipient?
  • Cancel felt finalEveryone thought cancelling would delete the whole transfer. But a new contact they'd added would stay saved.
  • How fresh is the rate?Everyone saw when the watchlist was last updated, but expected it to refresh at different times: every hour, at each log-in, or at the end of the day.

Act III

The work.

What I changed

Overall, the feedback was positive. Based on what people struggled with, I made some quick changes to the flow:

  1. A simpler tour. People mistook the tour for the real screen and started tapping on it, so I made it visually simpler.
  2. A clearer review screen. I cleaned up the review and confirm screen, and added a "Fee" line so the cost is obvious before you send.
  3. Copy edits. I edited the copy across the flow to make it easier to use.

What we noted for later

Some ideas were bigger than a quick fix, so we logged them to explore:

  • Rate alertsTwo people wanted to set a target rate, and send when it's reached.
  • Addresses for every countryAddress formats are different around the world. A Pakistani address, for example, has a sector, and our form had no field for it.
  • Tracking without relying on colourOne person said the yellow "in progress" colour could be hard to see for people with colour blindness, and a stepper that ticks off each step might be clearer.

Trust is built in the small confirmations.

Learnings

What I'm taking with me.

  1. Small details build trust. When it's people's money, things like showing the fee and clear copy matter as much as the big features.
  2. Test while you build. Testing during development meant we could make changes before launch, not after.
  3. Think global from the start. Bank codes, address formats and time zones all change from country to country, so the design has to plan for them early.
